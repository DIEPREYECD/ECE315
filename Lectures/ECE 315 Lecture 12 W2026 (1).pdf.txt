Clocks; Offsets, Drifts, Aging...
· Many electronics devices make use of crystal resonators
· Inexpensive, robust, well-understood
· Designated as "XO"
· TCXO ­ Temperature-Compensated XO; simple, inexpensive, good accuracy · OCXO ­ Oven-Controlled XO; complex, quite expensive, very accurate · VCXO ­ Voltage-Controlled XO; complex, expensive, accuracy depends on
external systems
· Offsets (tolerance), drifts, and aging are measured in parts-per-million (ppm) or even parts-per-billion (ppb)

Clocks; Offsets, Drifts...
· A clock with nominal frequency fnom and tolerance of N ppm will operate in the range [fnom - fnom * N/1e6, fnom + fnom * N/1e6]
· E.g., a 16 MHz clock with stability of 5 ppm will be between [15.99992, 16.00008] MHz
· A similar calculation tells us how the frequency varies across the XO's temperature range
· Usually the aging statistic is for 1 year. That means that the frequency will be fnom ± N ppm per year.
· The most aging happens over the first year, even the first months of operation

Specifying "bad" clocks
· Why would a standard specify a "bad" (poor) clock, one with a large ppm?
· Because they are very inexpensive
· 802.11, the basis of WiFi, specifies a receiver clock should be at least (no better than) 20 ppm
· It relies on clock recovery by the receiver, which is just software and the algorithms are well-known
· This helps keep the physical costs of WiFi down, promoting the standard

Asynchronous Interfaces
· The transmitter and receiver sides use physically distinct and unrelated clock signals. fRX = fnom ± N ppm  fTX = fnom ± M ppm
· A simple (naiive) clock recovery scheme is to have the receiver clock frequency be M times the transmitter frequency.
· E.g. fRX = 16 fTX ; At the receiver the clock is divided by 16 and 1 of 16 possible phases can be selected to best match fTX
· Thus, the original transmitter clock can be approximated by the divideddown receiver clock.
· However, over time (not much) the estimated clock signal will drift away due to fRX  fTX

Semi-synchronous Interfaces

· Some aspects of data transfer are synchronized by a clock signal, others are not.
Key Characteristics
· Clock present but not continuous: A clock signal (or timing reference) is provided, but not for every bit. Instead, it may mark frames, characters, or intervals.
· Data timing partly self-timed: The receiver uses the provided timing cues plus its own internal timing to interpret data.
· Less strict timing than synchronous interfaces: The sender and receiver don't need to maintain perfect, continuous clock alignment.
· More timing structure than asynchronous interfaces: Unlike asynchronous protocols (e.g., UART), semi-synchronous protocols rely on shared timing information beyond simple start/stop bits.

Semi-synchronous Interfaces

Why use a semi-synchronous interface? · Reduces the complexity of maintaining a fully shared clock. · Allows more flexible bit timing than fully synchronous links. · Still avoids the overhead of (asynchronous) per-character start/stop bits. · Often more tolerant of clock drift than fully synchronous systems. Examples · Manchester-encoded links where the clock is embedded and recovered, not
transmitted as a separate line. · I²C-like protocols where a clock exists but edges may pause or stretch. · WiFi, Bluetooth, ZigBee, LoRa

RS-232C Serial Transmission Standard

· RS-232C (1960) was originally intended to be a low-cost, asynchronous serial interface standard for connecting large central computers and remote terminal equipment to modems for the purposes of setting up data communication links over the public switched telephone network (PSTN)
· It was, and still is, used to connect DTE (data terminal equipment) such as a computer terminal or PC, and a DCE (data circuitterminating equipment or data communication equipment), such as a modem
· "modem" is short for modulator-demodulator, a device that converts digital signals to analogue signals for transmission, then converts them back

RS-232C
· A minimal RS-232C connection requires only three wires:
· (1) transmit data, (2) receive data, and a (3) ground return.
· RS-232C assumes that the data will be sent as 7-bit or 8-bit character codes, plus other "overhead bits" that provide timing, minimum character spacing, and (optionally) single-bit error detection using parity bits.
· RS-232C signals are single-ended, unmodulated signals.

RS-232C Alternatives

· Official RS-232C specification has serious limitations:
· The maximum transmission distance is only 15 m (50 ft.).
· The maximum bit/baud rate is 20,000 bits per second (up to 200 Kbps is possible, but outside of the spec.)
· Does not define such elements as the character encoding (i.e. ASCII, EBCDIC, or others), the framing of characters (start or stop bits, etc.), transmission order of bits, or error detection protocols.
· The standard did not foresee the requirements of devices such as computers, printers, test instruments, POS terminals, and so on,
· Remains in use in industrial applications and for embedded debugging, for example

RS-232C Alternatives
· RS-422 is a balanced (differential mode) serial transmission standard with higher maximum bit rates.
· Transmits +/- 6 V down to +/- 2 V differential outputs. · Input signals can be attenuated down to +/- 0.2 V. · Differential signals reject common mode additive noise. · Transmits up to 90 Kbps over up to 1.2 Km (3,900 ft).
· RS-485 is a balanced (differential mode) serial transmission standard with higher maximum bit rates. E.g. 2 Mb/s @ 50 m
· Supports multi-drop busses using tri-stateable drivers

Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter
· A peripheral device for asynchronous serial communication in which the data format and transmission speeds are configurable.
· Data bits are sent one by one (serially), from the least significant to the most significant, framed by start and stop bits so that precise timing is handled by the communication channel.
· The electric signaling levels are handled by a driver circuit external to the UART. Common signal levels are RS-232, RS-485, and raw transistor-transistor logic (TTL) for short debugging links.

Zynq-7000 SoC ­ Two UARTs

· Each supports independent, fully-programmable, full-duplex serial comms. · Programmable baud rates · Data formats supported;
· 6, 7 or 8-bit data; odd, even, no parity; 1.0, 1.5 or 2.0 stop bits.
· Interrupt conditions can be programmed for each UART. · Can use polling or interrupts. · Three error conditions are detected in hardware by each receiver:
· (1) parity error; (2) framing error; and (3) overrun error.
· The transmit and receive paths are provided with 64-byte buffers, which provide elastic storage to decouple the CPUs from the UARTs
