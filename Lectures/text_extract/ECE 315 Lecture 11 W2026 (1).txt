What is Computer Interfacing?

• In this course we look at the low-level aspects of computer
  interfacing; i.e. interfacing of embedded computers
• It is very similar to what is done at higher-levels, non-embedded
  computing systems, except that at that level there are more utilities,
  more computational resources, and so on to help

• Thus, computer interfacing is about connecting embedded systems
  to external devices (sensors, actuators, computers).
• Its purpose is to enable data exchange, control, and monitoring

Which Interfaces?

• Serial Interfaces: UART, SPI, I²C — short-range, low data rate.

• Parallel Interfaces: Faster but use more pins (e.g., LCD data buses).

• Memory interfaces: Serial and Parallel, direct memory access (DMA)

• Wireless Interfaces: Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, Zigbee.

Serial Interfaces
• A serial interface is a communication mechanism that transfers data
  one bit at a time over a single or a small set of communication lines,
  typically between a microcontroller and a peripheral device such as a
  sensor, display, or another microcontroller
Key Characteristics
  • Bit-by-bit transmission: Data is sent sequentially rather than in parallel (all bits
    at once), which reduces the number of physical connections required.
  • Clock synchronization: Synchronous interfaces use a shared clock signal,
    while asynchronous interfaces rely on timing agreements between devices
  • Directionality: Can be unidirectional (one-way) or bidirectional (two-way
                                                                    communication)

Common Serial Interfaces in Embedded Systems

• UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter):
  • Asynchronous communication (no shared clock).
  • Typically used for debugging or communication between
    controllers and PCs.

• SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface):
  • Synchronous, full-duplex.
  • Often used for fast communication with sensors or memory chips.

Common Serial Interfaces in Embedded Systems

• I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit):
  • Synchronous, half-duplex.
  • Supports multiple masters and slaves on the same bus.

• CAN (Controller Area Network):
  • Used in automotive and industrial systems.
  • Allows multiple microcontrollers to communicate without a central
    host.

• There are three common modes
• Simplex – data travels in only one direction, ever
• Half-Duplex – data travels in two directions, but never at the same time
• Full-Duplex – data travels in two direction simultaneously

The Nature of Signals in Interfaces

• Like to think of our world as being digital…

• Real world is always analogue, so we must deal with the nature and
  limitations of analogue signals before we think of digital data

• The aim is to detect information that is present in the analogue
  domain.
• To do this, we implement hardware and software to process the
  signals.

Analogue Signal Management and Processing

1) Analog signal buffering, conditioning and filtering
   • Use buffers/drivers to amplify signals & reduce output impedance
   • Noise rejection filters, anti-aliasing filters prior to analogue-to-digital
     conversion (A/D or ADC)


2) Impedance matching to avoid unwanted signal “ringing” (oscillations)
   • Avoid sudden changes in characteristic impedance of connections
   • Use a parallel termination resistance at the far end of a wire
   • Use a series termination resistance at the near end of a wire

Analogue Signal Management and Processing

3) Analog-to-digital (ADC) and digital-to-analog signal conversion (DAC)
   • ADC – Analog inputs from sensors need to be filtered, sampled and converted
     into digital form to permit digital signal processing (DSP)
   • DAC – Digital outputs need to be converted into voltage or current signals to
     control actuators, heaters, light sources, radio signals, etc.


4) Digital signal level conversion and signal isolation
   • Voltage level conversion (3v3 ➔ 5v), voltage-current conversion
   • Optical isolation transceivers, isolation transformers

Digital Signal Management and Processing

5) Digital modulation of an analog signal
  • Digital signals must be encoded as analog signals of controlled bandwidth to
    propagate well through the passband of a communication channel
  • Modem = modulator (in transmitter) + demodulator (in receiver)

6) Timing recovery, demodulation & decoding a modulated signal.
  • Steps that are present in many digital communication receivers.
  • Error-correcting codes are used to detect and correct digital errors.




                                                Adapted from ECE 315 W2025 by Dr. Bruce Cockburn   17




                                                                                                        17
Digital Signal Management and Processing

7) Synchronization, framing, handshaking, error detection
  • Start (for synchronization) and stop bits (for spacing) in RS-232C
  • Request-to-send and clear-to-send handshaking (for flow control)
  • Parity bit (or checksum) generation at the transmitter
  • Parity bit (or checksum) re-generation & checking at the receiver.
  • One parity bit permits the detection of an odd number of bit errors.
8) Data buffering and flow control to handle rate fluctuations
  • Temporary storage in buffers inside transmitters and receivers
  • A buffer accommodates short-term data rate fluctuations, but flow control is
    required to handle longer-term mismatches in data rates.

Unbalanced (or Single-ended) Digital Signalling
• A single-ended signal comprises two wires, one that carries the
  signal, the other that is the reference for the signal
  • Usually, the second wire is set to or defined as ground
• Advantages: The reference wire can be shared for multiple signals.
• Disadvantages: Noise added to the signal and/or the ground
  reference (e.g., ground loop noise) can cause
  bit errors at the receiver

Balanced (or Differential) Digital Signaling
• Two wires – no ground (GND) is needed
• The transmitter sends two signals; V+ and the inverse signal, V—
                                    V— = V+
• The receiver subtracts the two to recover the single-ended signal
• If V+ and V— are corrupted by common-mode additive noise, Vnoise,
  then the noise is cancelled out by subtraction:
                (V+ + Vnoise) – (V— + Vnoise) = V+ — V— = 2 V+

Advantage: Common-mode noise is cancelled at a differential receiver.

Unwanted Signals Across Systems
• Noise, DC offsets, cross-talk (strong signals are coupled into circuits)
  can wreak havoc with digital (and audio systems)
• DC Offset: A constant voltage ( ± ) added to a signal, shifting its
  baseline away from zero.
   • Caused by ground loops, sensor bias, or mismatched circuits.
   • Leads to distortion, reduced dynamic range, and inaccurate measurements.
• Noise: Random, unwanted variations superimposed on a signal.
   • Sources include power supply ripple, electromagnetic interference (EMI), and
     digital switching.
   • Can obscure weak signals and reduce signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

                                                         Made with help from ChatGPT   24

Effects of DC Offsets and Noise

• Measurement Errors: DC offsets bias analog-to-digital converters
  (ADCs), leading to incorrect readings.
• Signal Distortion: Noise and offsets alter waveform integrity,
  especially in audio and sensor systems.
• Ground Loop Problems: Differences in ground potential introduce hum
  or DC currents through shared grounds.
• System Instability: Feedback systems can behave unpredictably if
  offsets or noise corrupt reference signals.
