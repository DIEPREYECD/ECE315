In 500 words or less, describe the RFID reader hard real-time constraint discussed in lecture 6. How would you manage this using FreeRTOS?

Your Answer:

In Lecture 6, the RFID reader example has a clear hard real-time constraint. When the RFID chip triggers an interrupt, I have about 30 microseconds to respond. If I don’t respond within that window, the tag “forgets” and the communication fails. That’s why it’s hard real-time, missing the deadline doesn’t just slow the system down, it actually breaks the protocol and the read operation fails. The MCU communicates with the RFID chip over SPI, and the interrupt signals that the next step in the handshake must happen immediately.

If I were managing this using FreeRTOS, I would design the system to minimize latency. First, I would keep the interrupt service routine (ISR) extremely short. When the RFID interrupt fires, my ISR would do the absolute minimum, like just notify a high-priority task using a direct-to-task notification. I would avoid doing long SPI transactions inside the ISR because that increases jitter and makes timing less predictable.

Then I would create a dedicated high-priority RFID task that blocks waiting for that notification. As soon as it is unblocked, it would immediately perform the required SPI communication with the RFID chip. Giving this task a very high priority ensures it preempts lower-priority tasks and runs right away.

Finally, I would make sure the rest of the system doesn’t interfere. That means keeping other ISRs short, avoiding long critical sections, and not overloading the CPU. Leaving some idle time in the system gives me headroom so I can reliably meet the 30 µs deadline every time.

Clocks green and red have a frequency of 10 MHz ±10 ppm and are started in perfect synchronization.

What is the shortest time before they will be exactly out of synchronization; that is, when their clock pulses are opposite phase as shown below?

State your assumptions and show your calculations and express your answer in seconds, minutes, hours, and days.

Your Answer:

We are given that both clocks start perfectly synchronized, so their phase difference is 0 at t = 0. To get the shortest time before they become exactly out of sync (180° out of phase), I assume the worst-case tolerance:

  • one clock runs at +10 ppm and the other runs at −10 ppm (maximum possible frequency difference).

The nominal frequency is:

f0 = 10 MHz = 10 × 106 Hz

So the nominal period is:

T0 = 1 / f0 = 1 / (10 × 106) = 1 × 10−7 s = 100 ns

"Exactly out of synchronization" means the clocks are 180° out of phase, which is a half-cycle time shift:

Δt = T0/2 = 50 ns

Now compute the worst-case frequencies:

ffast = 10 MHz(1 + 10 ppm) = 107(1 + 10−5) = 10,000,100 Hz
fslow = 10 MHz(1 − 10 ppm) = 107(1 − 10−5) = 9,999,900 Hz

The maximum frequency difference is:

Δf = ffast − fslow = 200 Hz

A frequency difference of Δf means the relative phase drifts by Δf cycles per second. To become opposite phase, the relative phase must change by 0.5 cycle, so:

t = 0.5 / Δf = 0.5 / 200 = 0.0025 s

Final Answer (shortest time):

  • Seconds: t = 0.0025 s
  • Minutes: t = 0.0025/60 = 4.17 × 10−5 min
  • Hours: t = 0.0025/3600 = 6.94 × 10−7 hr
  • Days: t = 0.0025/86400 = 2.89 × 10−8 days

So, the shortest time for the clocks to become exactly opposite phase is about 2.5 ms.

If a clock has an accuracy of ±10 ppm and is set to exactly Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), what is the minimum time it will take to drift at least 10 seconds from the true, UTC time? Show your calculations and express your answer in seconds, minutes, hours, and days.

Your Answer:

Given: Clock accuracy = ±10 ppm, and the clock is initially set exactly to UTC.

Assumption (minimum time): To drift away from true UTC as fast as possible, I assume the clock is at the worst-case limit of its tolerance, i.e., it runs 10 ppm fast (or 10 ppm slow). 

Meaning of ppm:

10 ppm = 10 × 10−6 = 1 × 10−5

This means the clock can accumulate time error at a rate of:

(1 × 10−5) seconds of error per 1 second of real time

We want: time error ≥ 10 seconds.

Let t be the real elapsed time. Then the worst-case accumulated error is:

error = (10 ppm) × t = (1 × 10−5)t

Set this equal to 10 seconds to find the minimum t:

(1 × 10−5)t = 10
t = 10 / (1 × 10−5) = 1,000,000 s

Convert units:

  • Seconds: 1,000,000 s
  • Minutes: 1,000,000 / 60 = 16,666.67 min
  • Hours: 1,000,000 / 3600 = 277.78 hr
  • Days: 1,000,000 / 86400 = 11.57 days

Final Answer: The minimum time to drift at least 10 seconds from UTC is 1,000,000 s ≈ 16,666.67 min ≈ 277.78 hr ≈ 11.57 days.
